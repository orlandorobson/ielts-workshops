# IELTS Writing Task 2 — Continuous workshop build specification

Version 1.0 · 3 November 2026  
Status: concrete implementation brief; no website changes made  
Priority: the user's latest sequence and simplicity requirements supersede earlier proposals for lengthy planning units.

## 1. The learner journey

**Understand the question → Plan the structure → Introduction → Main paragraphs → Conclusion.**

After the five teaching sections, show the learner's complete answer for a short check and one useful revision. Independent practice is a quiet optional handoff, not a sixth compulsory teaching course.

One public entry at the existing `/writing/task-2/` URL. Continuous scrolling, clear section boundaries and a discreet section selector. Students can resume a section or return to writing. Do not turn every learning moment into a separate screen, compulsory checkpoint or progress gate.

The learner carries WT2-W06, the online-courses question, through planning and writing. Other canonical questions provide brief contrasts where necessary. Avoid restarting the learner's work whenever the teaching example changes.

## 2. The teaching promise

Learner-facing wording:

> You do not need a perfect plan or perfect sentences to get started. Understand the question, put related ideas together, and help the reader follow your answer.

For the recommended paragraph shape:

> A useful way to build a paragraph is to start with its main point, explain it, and add support. Finish when the point is clear. Sometimes one last sentence helps; sometimes the paragraph is already finished.

Teacher-facing distinction: IELTS assesses relevant task response, development, organisation and language. It does not prescribe our named three-part paragraph formula, exactly two paragraphs, a fixed sentence count or a separate closure sentence. These are useful teaching options, not additional assessment rules. Paragraphing and logical organisation themselves do matter: do not imply that all structure is optional. Reference: [official Task 2 descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf).

Students should learn by noticing, making a small decision and writing. They should not have to memorise the terminology or complete an exhaustive planning method before they can begin.

## 3. Understand the question

**One focus:** what must this answer do?

1. Show a short canonical prompt. Ask learners to identify the two jobs or the required decision in simple language.
2. Contrast only the instruction lines needed for the teaching point. In particular distinguish causes from problems; advantages/disadvantages from outweigh; evaluation from reasons-plus-evaluation.
3. Return to the learner's own prompt. Display its required jobs in a small, accessible reminder beside the writing.

For WT2-W06: **Explain why adults choose online courses. Decide whether the change is positive or negative.**

Keep the familiar essay-type labels secondary. One correct label is not evidence that the learner understands the actions. Help should clarify the question without supplying an entire answer.

## 4. Plan the structure — quick and obvious

**One focus:** which ideas belong together?

Learner-facing instruction:

> What will each main paragraph do? Put ideas that belong together in the same group. A few words are enough.

Start with an obvious structure, not a brainstorming maze. Show two short plan cards and let learners judge **Cool / Splat**. Give immediate, brief feedback about task coverage or grouping. Use one straightforward case and one revealing contrast, then move to the learner's plan. Additional examples are optional.

### First contrast: causes and solutions

Use WT2-W07, city noise.

**Cool plan**

- Paragraph 1: causes — traffic, construction.
- Paragraph 2: solutions — control noisy vehicles; limit building-work hours.

**Splat plan**

- Paragraph 1: traffic noise.
- Paragraph 2: construction noise.

Feedback: the second plan groups two causes sensibly, but leaves no place for the required solutions. It would need revision to answer this question. Do not teach it as universally bad organisation independent of its prompt.

### Second contrast: advantages and disadvantages

Use WT2-W03, cashless payments.

**Cool plan:** Paragraph 1 — convenience and spending records; Paragraph 2 — access difficulties and failed payments.

**Splat plan:** Paragraph 1 — advantages of cards; Paragraph 2 — advantages of phones. Both groups provide benefits; the requested drawbacks are missing.

### Useful structures across the bank

| Demand | A dependable starting structure | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Causes / solutions | Causes; solutions | Connect measures to the identified causes |
| Problems / solutions | Problems; solutions | Do not substitute causes for the requested problems |
| Advantages / disadvantages | Benefits; drawbacks | No compulsory outweigh judgement |
| Discuss both views + opinion | One view; the other view | Make the writer's own view clear too |
| Outweigh | One side; the other side | Explain why one carries more weight; not a counting exercise |
| Agreement | Two connected reasons for the chosen position | A qualified or alternative organisation can also work |
| Positive / negative development | Two contributions supporting the judgement | A benefit/drawback comparison is an option, not the only plan |
| Reasons + evaluation | Reasons; evaluation | Avoid repeating the same benefit without showing its different job |
| Causes + effects | Reasons; effects | Solutions are not a substitute for effects |

Do not show this whole table as a compulsory learner checklist. It is authoring guidance for loading a relevant starting point for each question.

### The learner's plan

For WT2-W06 provide only:

- **Paragraph 1 — Why?** Short idea notes.
- **Paragraph 2 — Positive or negative?** Short idea notes.
- **My view:** positive / negative / mixed, with an optional few words explaining it.

The view field appears only when relevant to the demand. Two paragraph groups are the recommended default, not a rigid content limit. Learners can edit the groups or change their mind while writing. Do not require a topic sentence, explanation, example, closure and reflection for each group at the planning stage.

No long plan assessment, ranking exercise, compulsory justification, word minimum or completion gate. If someone is stuck, offer two possible idea fragments and a hint, not a full essay plan by default.

## 5. Introductions — achievable, then optional expansion

**One focus:** introduce this question and make the needed answer direction clear.

### Teaching sequence

1. **See what works:** show a small Cool / Spicy / Splat set for the same prompt. Cool works in straightforward language; Spicy adds useful range without changing the answer; Splat misses a defined job.
2. **Decide:** learners react to short introductions and see why each works or needs repair. Include a plausible near-success, not only an absurd distractor. A grammatical introduction can still miss the question.
3. **Build a Cool introduction:** order meaningful word clusters. Preserve chunks such as a main clause or useful phrase; do not make this a puzzle about individual function words.
4. **Optional Spicy build:** another cluster arrangement expressing a useful contrast, qualification or relationship. It is not the required upgrade from Cool.
5. **Write an introduction:** use the learner's own question and plan. Help stays optional; a short topic-and-view hint or a phrase can appear beside the field.

Use a brief demonstration first when the learner does not yet understand the job. Attempt-first is encouraged when the learner is ready, not enforced universally.

### Opinion and agreement distinctions

| Prompt demand | What the introduction should make clear in this workshop |
|---|---|
| Agree/disagree; discuss both views and give an opinion; outweigh; positive/negative | A relevant position or evaluative direction, preferably stated early |
| Reasons plus positive/negative | Both the reason task and the writer's judgement |
| Causes/solutions; problems/solutions; causes/effects; advantages/disadvantages | The question's required jobs and response direction; no forced “I agree” or unrequested outweigh verdict |

IELTS expects a relevant, developed response/position. “Explicitly state the opinion in the introduction” is our dependable recommendation where a judgement is requested, not a claim that a particular sentence or placement is prescribed by IELTS. For explanatory tasks, the response still needs a coherent answer, even when no personal-opinion statement is requested.

An introduction need not contain examples, full reasoning, a detailed essay map or a fixed number of sentences. Do not require every task word to be replaced. Preserve meaning and permit useful repetition of key nouns.

## 6. Main paragraphs — one usable process, different content

**One focus:** make a main point and help the reader understand it.

Learners should recognise that the two main paragraphs can be built in the same broad way. Their content and purpose change with the question; the writer does not need to learn a separate paragraph formula for every essay type.

Use three visible teaching moves:

1. **Main point:** what is this paragraph saying?
2. **Develop it:** why, how, what happens, or what example helps?
3. **Finish if needed:** is there one useful final connection, or is the point already complete?

Introduce “topic sentence,” “development” and “closure” as optional names after learners understand these jobs. A separate example is not compulsory in every paragraph; relevant explanation or other support can develop the point. A closing comparison, consequence or final supporting detail may already finish it. Do not ask for a mini-conclusion or repeated topic sentence just to fill a third box.

### Core practice sequence

1. Read one short Cool paragraph and notice what each part contributes. Include a paragraph that ends successfully without an extra closure sentence.
2. Judge a small number of contrasting paragraphs or fragments. Explain the specific Splat and compare a local Cool repair.
3. Try one repair. Preserve the writer's idea; do not replace it with a new expert argument.
4. Write the learner's first paragraph with the question and short plan available.
5. Use the same process for the second paragraph with less repeated instruction. Both paragraph contributions belong in the complete-essay journey; they are not permanently optional merely because one is practised in detail first.
6. Offer an optional Spicy comparison or another attempt. Useful spice is a clearer relationship, qualification or precise example, not difficult vocabulary for its own sake.

### Student-grounded Splat library

Use the local source study and evidence register for provenance. No public real-student names are required. A label applies to the selected writing move, not the student's whole ability.

| Observed pattern | Short learner-facing explanation | Repair direction |
|---|---|---|
| Restatement after “For example” | “This says the same thing again. What real situation could show it?” | Supply a specific case, then connect it |
| Repeated claim in several forms | “The words change, but the point stays in the same place.” | Add one why/how/result step |
| Example with a missing link | “The situation is clear. How does it support the point?” | Make the relevant mechanism visible |
| A different solution introduced as an example | “This is another idea, not an example of the first one.” | Choose which proposal to develop |
| Too many ideas in one chain | “There is useful thinking here. Finish one point before adding the next.” | Select and separate connected steps |
| Topic discussion instead of the exact answer | “This is about the topic. Does it answer this question?” | Reconnect the idea to the demand |
| Abrupt final sentence | “Does the reader have enough to understand the point?” | Add the missing connection only if needed |

Each Splat item needs the actual question, a short extract, diagnosis, one repair opportunity and a local Cool comparison. Reveal only the feedback belonging to that item. Avoid making the learner read a full correction report before continuing.

Example from the accepted Aisha composite: “For example, they will know more things about the job and improve themself.” The first decision is whether a specific example has appeared. A local repair can name a skill and show how it helps at work. Do not merely correct “themself” and imply the paragraph-development problem is solved.

Successful local fixes are labelled teaching demonstrations. They do not replace or silently polish the frozen, deliberately imperfect whole-essay models.

## 7. Conclusions — two small tasks

**Task A: bring the main answer together.**

Show two ways to do this:

- Restate the main contributions in new, natural wording.
- Synthesise their shared meaning or implication.

These are alternatives or complementary options, not two compulsory conclusion sentences. Learners need not perform difficult synonym substitution. Reusing a precise key term is acceptable. Topic sentences or the short plan can act as a reminder of what has actually been argued.

**Task B: finish the judgement, when the question asks for one.**

For agreement, discussion-and-opinion, outweigh or positive/negative tasks, ensure the conclusion finishes the same position the essay developed. It may be integrated into Task A; a separate opinion sentence is not compulsory. For explanatory tasks, close the required answer without inventing an agreement statement.

### Teaching sequence

1. Compare a small set of endings: Cool synthesis, a workable but mechanical restatement, and a Splat that changes position or starts a new main argument.
2. Connect an ending to the actual paragraph points, not just to the topic.
3. Try a brief restatement or synthesis using the learner's own paragraph notes.
4. When relevant, check that the conclusion's judgement matches the introduction and paragraphs. Then write the short conclusion in the answer.

A mechanical restatement can be acceptable; do not classify it as Splat solely for lacking elegance. “In conclusion” is a useful option, not a required phrase. Do not prescribe an exact sentence count or penalise a conclusion for retaining important nouns.

## 8. Finish with the learner's whole answer

Assemble introduction, both paragraph contributions and conclusion in one editable answer. Keep the question available. Preserve the first assembled version before revision, and never overwrite later edits from stale component fields.

Three short checking prompts:

- Did I answer what the question asked?
- Can the reader follow my points and their support?
- What is one useful thing to improve now?

Allow one selected revision. Broader feedback and full fictional models are optional, contextual resources. Their examiner-style comments remain in the third person about the named fictional writer. Feedback on the learner's own work may address the learner normally.

## 9. Interaction and content rules

- Cool is a successful endpoint. Spicy is optional. Splat identifies a missed job and leads to repair.
- Votes are individual learning judgements with reasons in feedback, not proof that the majority determines quality. Do not display fabricated classroom totals. Aggregate voting is out of scope unless separately implemented.
- Do not use exact-match checking for open writing. Cluster tasks can recognise multiple grammatical arrangements when valid; offer comparison when automatic judgement would be unreliable.
- Keep option ordering stable after interaction. Randomise only where order has no semantic purpose, and save the order.
- No forced timers, answer streaks, completion gates or per-sentence error quotas. No fixed maximum drafting time; fast teaching should leave room for sustained writing.
- Preserve focus, scroll position and every writing field when checking a vote, opening support or moving a cluster. Keyboard movement controls accompany any drag interaction.
- Models remain hidden until requested or introduced at the relevant teaching moment. Full model essays must not appear automatically beside an unattempted full writing task.
- All nine canonical prompts and model responses remain frozen source content. New local teaching examples and repairs have separate IDs and labels.

## 11. Implementation order

1. Inspect the repository and map existing activities to the five sections: retain, simplify, optional or retire.
2. Integrate the frozen content and build the **Understand → Plan the structure** portion first in a local preview. Test the simpler planning experience before multiplying activities.
3. Build introductions: short judgements, Cool clusters, optional Spicy clusters, independent writing with help.
4. Build the shared paragraph process and a small, high-value Splat/repair set; use the same process for both paragraph contributions.
5. Build conclusions with main-answer synthesis and conditional judgement work.
6. Connect the assembled answer, preservation/migration, revision, optional models and verified practice handoff.
7. Review the full local release candidate and evidence before requesting deployment confirmation. No deployment is authorised by this specification.

## 12. Acceptance checks

- The learner's plan consists of two understandable groups and any needed view; no hidden exhaustive planning work or validation remains.
- Each central activity makes its purpose clear and feeds promptly into usable writing. Optional extensions do not inflate the core route.
- An opinion is taught when requested, without forcing “I agree” into explanatory tasks or inventing a fixed introduction formula.
- Cool and optional Spicy cluster builders preserve meaning; multiple valid arrangements are handled fairly.
- Main-point/development/optional-finish teaching does not become a compulsory three-part paragraph template or a fixed sentence count.
- At least one example ends without an additional closure sentence and is explicitly accepted.
- Splat feedback identifies the actual meaning/development issue and offers a repair path; it does not merely count errors or replace the learner's argument.
- Both paragraph contributions and a conclusion can become part of a complete editable answer; the endpoint does not quietly leave half the essay unwritten.
- Conclusion activities distinguish synthesis/restatement from an opinion judgement, without requiring both in separate sentences.
- The model bank is preserved and remains clearly fictional and estimated; whole models are not silently converted into flawless teaching text.
- All drafts, choices and valid cluster arrangements persist. Old entry points and storage migration are verified without deleting recoverable work.
- Check keyboard access, phone/tablet/laptop layouts, sustained typing, stable focus, scroll and support disclosures. No visible typing or navigation stalls.
- Pilot whether learners can make and use a plan more quickly and with less confusion. Do not claim that shorter scrolling time itself proves better writing.
