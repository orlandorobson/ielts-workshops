# IELTS Workshops — Design System

**Version:** 1.3  
**Status:** Foundation specification

## 1. Design Purpose

IELTS Workshops should feel like a contemporary adult learning environment rather than a digitised worksheet, conventional LMS or generic educational website.

The visual system should be:

- adult;
- calm;
- contemporary;
- elegant;
- highly legible;
- spacious;
- purposeful;
- visually distinctive without being distracting.

The design should support concentration and learning.

Professional does not mean corporate.

Educational does not mean childish.

Simple does not mean generic.

Visual interest should come from composition, typography, space, meaningful colour, instructional graphics and occasional illustration rather than constant decoration.


# 2. Space Is Part of the Design

Do not fill space merely because it is available.

The project is not constrained by the economics or physical dimensions of photocopied A4 materials.

A screen may legitimately contain:

- one important question;
- one graph;
- one short instruction;
- one figure;
- one writing activity;
- or a small amount of carefully positioned content.

Generous negative space is desirable when it improves hierarchy, focus or calm.

Do not compress several learning stages onto one screen merely to reduce page length.

At the same time, avoid unnecessary scrolling where information genuinely needs to be considered together.


# 3. Overall Visual Direction

The broad visual direction is:

**CONTEMPORARY EDITORIAL MINIMALISM**

The intended visual language combines:

- elegant minimalist editorial illustration;
- simplified geometric human forms;
- adult natural proportions;
- restrained architectural or environmental illustration;
- gentle colour fields;
- selective stronger colour moments;
- clean information graphics;
- subtle depth;
- generous whitespace.

The visual character may draw inspiration from sophisticated editorial illustration and simplified mid-century architectural composition, but must not directly imitate any particular artwork, television title sequence, illustrator or copyrighted visual identity.

The goal is a coherent original visual language.


# 4. Avoid Generic EdTech Aesthetics

Do not default to common educational or software-template conventions.

Avoid:

- cartoon characters;
- anime styling;
- childish illustration;
- exaggerated facial expressions;
- oversized heads and tiny bodies;
- corporate clip-art characters;
- artificial stock-image diversity scenes;
- excessive rounded cards;
- every section being placed inside a box;
- unnecessary gradients;
- glowing buttons;
- glassmorphism;
- excessive drop shadows;
- decorative dashboards;
- achievement badges without pedagogical purpose;
- visual gamification without learning value;
- random educational icons;
- illustrations of smiling people using laptops merely to fill space.

A page should not look like a generic template with IELTS content inserted into it.


# 5. Visual Rhythm

Not every screen should have the same composition.

Use variation deliberately.

A workshop may move between:

- a figure on a quiet background;
- a text-led screen;
- an instructional diagram;
- a task visual;
- a full or partial scene;
- a writing workspace;
- a model investigation;
- an almost completely neutral independent-practice screen.

This creates rhythm without requiring decorative complexity.

Repeated pedagogical interactions should remain behaviourally consistent even when surrounding compositions vary.

The platform should not use exactly the same colour atmosphere throughout every learning stage. Major learning phases may use gently differentiated colour families to create orientation, visual rhythm, progression and cognitive separation between different kinds of work.

Use this relationship:

**MAJOR LEARNING PHASE → SUBTLE COLOUR ATMOSPHERE**

**INDIVIDUAL ACTIVITY → INHERITS THAT ATMOSPHERE**

Do not assign a strong new colour to every activity. Do not create a rainbow interface, use colour as gamification or make colour the only signal of a stage. Colour should support navigation subconsciously rather than demand attention.


# 6. Colour Philosophy

Colour should organise and support the learning experience.

Prefer:

- gentle background colour fields;
- warm neutrals;
- muted natural colours;
- restrained accent colours;
- occasional stronger colour moments.

Possible colour families may include:

- warm sand;
- stone;
- muted ochre;
- terracotta;
- dusty blue;
- deep teal;
- soft olive;
- warm off-white;
- pale grey.

These are directions, not final colour tokens.

Background treatments should normally use very pale versions of these families. More saturated versions may be used sparingly for small heading rules, active controls, selected states, icons, progress markers and other small structural accents.

Do not choose the final palette until it has been tested visually in the prototype.

Colour may be used to:

- distinguish major learning sections;
- signal interaction;
- identify support;
- distinguish feedback states;
- create visual rhythm;
- help learners understand grouping or relationships.

Do not use colour merely to make a screen busier.

Avoid presenting too many strong colours simultaneously.

Colour zones communicate place within a learning journey. They must never imply score, correctness, quality or achievement.


# 7. Grouped Backgrounds

Related learning stages may share a gentle background family.

This can help learners perceive that several screens belong to the same conceptual stage.

For example, an introduction sequence may use one subtle background family and an overview sequence another.

This should remain understated.

Do not create a rainbow sequence in which every screen receives a different colour.

Colour grouping should support conceptual grouping.

The exact mapping may vary by workshop. A useful starting direction for Task 2 is:

- **Understand the question:** cool dusty blue or blue-grey;
- **Build the answer:** muted olive or sage;
- **Organise and write:** warm sand or restrained ochre;
- **Check and revise:** muted terracotta or stone-purple.

This is not an immutable platform-wide colour code. Different workshops may have different phase structures. The permanent principle is:

**GENTLE VISUAL CHANGE → ORIENTATION AND RHYTHM**

not:

**COLOUR → SCORE OR CORRECTNESS**


# 8. Visual Guidance and Independence

Where appropriate, visual density may decrease as scaffolding decreases.

Supported learning can contain more visible guidance, annotations and contextual cues.

Independent performance should become visually quieter and closer to the actual task.

This can reinforce the pedagogical movement from support towards independence.

Do not remove information that the learner genuinely needs merely to create this effect.


# 9. Typography

Typography should be highly legible and appropriate for extended adult learning.

The typographic system should feel contemporary and neutral-to-editorial rather than corporate or playful.

Prioritise:

- excellent screen readability;
- clear hierarchy;
- comfortable reading sizes;
- generous line spacing;
- strong distinction between instructional text and learner content;
- readable long-form passages;
- clear numerals for charts and data;
- reliable rendering across common devices.

Do not select final typefaces until they can be visually tested.

Avoid decorative display fonts for normal instructional content.

Typography should carry much of the visual hierarchy so that boxes, lines and colours do not have to.


# 10. Illustration System

Illustration is an important part of the visual identity but is not required on every screen.

Its limited use in the current prototypes must not become the permanent visual model for future workshops.

Use the smallest visual intervention that achieves the purpose.

The preferred broad visual language is **contemporary editorial illustration** rather than photorealistic stock imagery, lifestyle advertising, corporate illustration, generic EdTech cartoons, tourism advertising or highly detailed AI world-building.

This is not one rigid house cartoon style. Human scenes, landscapes, industrial subjects, processes, maps and data visuals may require different treatments. Consistency should come from shared design canons, palette discipline, composition quality and visual judgement—not from making every image look identical.

The core rule is:

> **Illustrate the situation, not the instruction.**

Good uses include establishing a location, showing the people and social context involved in a task, giving a new scenario a visual identity or marking an occasional transition between major phases.

Avoid generic people thinking, pointing at text or celebrating a correct answer; decorative laptops beside writing activities; teacher/student clip art; and illustration added merely because an area looks empty.

Do not illustrate every screen. A preferred rhythm is:

**CONTEXT / ILLUSTRATED ENTRY → CLEAN THINKING WORKSPACE → JUDGEMENT / CONSTRUCTION → WRITING WORKSPACE → OCCASIONAL NEW CONTEXT VISUAL**

Large areas of clean space remain desirable. Illustration should contrast with the functional learning interface, and its scarcity is part of its value.

Three broad illustration scales are available.


## 10.1 Figure

One person, or two people interacting.

The background may consist only of:

- a colour field;
- a simple geometric shadow;
- a small number of environmental cues;
- or empty space.

This is appropriate when human presence or communication matters but a full environment does not.


## 10.2 Scene

A simplified environment such as:

- a contemporary workspace;
- classroom;
- university;
- home;
- café;
- reception area;
- office;
- public or social setting.

Scenes should use restrained architectural perspective and selective detail.

Do not render every object.

The scene should establish context without competing with the learning content.


## 10.3 Figure + Scene

Use people within a simplified environment when the relationship between the person and context is meaningful.

Examples may include:

- a learner working at a computer;
- neighbours meeting;
- colleagues talking;
- a customer speaking to a receptionist;
- people discussing an issue.

Do not create a full scene when a simple figure would communicate the idea adequately.


## 10.4 Minimum Environment

Use the minimum environment the image needs.

A context illustration may consist of:

- one person on a transparent background;
- one person against a restrained colour field;
- a small group with no detailed setting;
- a person with one or two environmental cues;
- a fuller environment only when the environment itself matters.

Do not invent artwork, decorative plants, ornaments, extensive furniture, architectural details, lifestyle objects or background people merely to make an image appear complete.

Every visible element should do at least one of these jobs:

1. establish the learning context;
2. support the person's believable activity;
3. clarify the learning subject;
4. improve the necessary composition.

If it does none of these, remove it.

The core principle is:

> **CONTEXT, NOT WORLD-BUILDING.**


# 11. Illustration Style

The preferred direction is:

> **EDITORIAL, NOT HOUSE CARTOON.**

Human illustration may generally use:

- adult natural proportions;
- simplified geometric forms;
- elegant silhouettes;
- restrained facial detail;
- natural poses;
- flat or lightly textured vector treatment;
- clean shapes;
- limited colour palettes;
- subtle depth;
- minimal or no cartoon outlining.

People should appear recognisably human without photorealism.

Avoid:

- anime;
- children's-book illustration;
- caricature;
- glamour illustration;
- fashion-advertising poses;
- exaggerated musculature;
- sexualised representation;
- artificial constant smiling;
- corporate vector clip art.

The desired result is closer to intelligent editorial illustration than educational cartooning. Moderately detailed editorial vector, simplified flat vector, silhouette, cropped portrait, environmental composition, geometric landscape and abstracted industrial treatments may all belong to the same system when appropriate.

Do not standardise every person into identical facial proportions, eyes, noses, bodies, line widths or rendering formulas. Individual illustrations may vary while following the same canons.


## 11.1 Shape Before Detail

Build an illustration first through:

1. silhouette;
2. gesture;
3. crop;
4. large colour fields;
5. negative space;
6. visual hierarchy.

Only then add smaller details. Fine detail must not compensate for weak composition.

Use the **Blur Test**: imagine the image heavily blurred so that small details disappear. The major subject, major colour relationships, important gesture, spatial hierarchy and visual balance should still be perceptible. If the composition fails when blurred, adding detail will not solve it.


## 11.2 Gesture Creates Meaning

Prefer meaningful human gesture and orientation over explanatory background detail. Eye-line, hand movement, leaning, listening posture, writing, pointing, holding, turning and body orientation can communicate the activity or relationship more effectively than a constructed environment.

A useful gesture can replace an entire explanatory background.


## 11.3 Crop With Confidence

The frame is a composition, not a container.

Do not automatically fit the entire person, desk, chair, room or every object inside the frame. Cropped bodies, partial furniture, objects extending beyond edges, strong close framing and asymmetric composition are valid when they strengthen visual focus.


## 11.4 Colour Is Part of the Composition

Colour should not merely decorate completed line work. Use it structurally through relationships between skin, clothing, background, subject, negative space and workshop phase atmosphere.

Strong clothing colour may function as a major graphic shape. A background colour field may provide both context and composition.

Do not make the complete visual system beige, grey or desaturated merely because the learners are adults.

> **ADULT ≠ COLOURLESS.**


## 11.5 Remove Competition, Not Necessarily Detail

Minimalism does not require every image to become a silhouette. Useful detail may remain when it supports this hierarchy:

**PRIMARY SUBJECT → ACTION / RELATIONSHIP → NECESSARY CONTEXT → SUBORDINATE DETAIL**

If everything has equal visual importance, simplify.


## 11.6 Subject Determines Visual Treatment

Do not force every subject through the same illustration formula.

- For human scenes, gesture, relationship, posture and human presence may dominate.
- For landscapes, space, horizon, atmosphere and scale may dominate.
- For industrial subjects, geometry, repetition, silhouette, scale and spatial organisation may dominate.
- For processes, sequence and transformation dominate.
- For maps, spatial clarity dominates.
- For data, information accuracy dominates.

Apply the shared visual canons according to what makes the subject meaningful.


## 11.7 Preserve the Subject's Spatial Character

Simplification must not remove the spatial quality that makes a subject visually meaningful.

A conversation may depend on eye-line, proximity and gesture. A landscape may depend on emptiness, horizon and scale. An industrial complex may depend on enormous horizontal extent, repeated vertical structures, surrounding open space and distance.

Do not make a subject more visually dramatic if doing so destroys its essential spatial character.

> **Scale does not always come from making the subject large. Sometimes scale comes from showing how much space surrounds it.**


# 12. Environmental Illustration

The core environmental rule is:

> **SUGGEST PLACE; DON'T CONSTRUCT IT.**

A setting normally needs only enough information for the learner to understand the situation. A background may be a colour field, a few geometric forms, one architectural clue, one domestic clue, a simplified horizon or transparency.

Architectural and environmental scenes may use:

- simplified perspective;
- translucent or lightly layered planes;
- restrained geometry;
- selective furniture and objects;
- gentle shadows;
- broad colour areas;
- line work that can fade into the page.

Transparency may be used subtly within illustration and composition.

Do not use fashionable glassmorphism as a general interface style.

Illustrated environments should feel contemporary rather than futuristic or nostalgic unless a particular learning context requires otherwise.

Do not construct an entire fictional world merely because an image generator can. Realistic brickwork, signage, benches, architectural history, crowds, detailed landscaping and other setting information are unnecessary unless the subject or learning task needs them.


## 12.1 One Action, Few Objects

When showing an activity, use the minimum useful set of objects needed to make that activity legible.

- Study may need only a person and notebook.
- Computer study may need a person, laptop and notebook.
- Two students studying normally need one laptop and one or two books or notebooks.
- Working from home may need only a person and laptop.
- Conversation may need only two people, eye-line and gesture.

Do not create object piles as visual shorthand. Five books do not communicate “student” better than one useful book.


## 12.2 Non-Human and Industrial Reference Lesson

The refinery experiments demonstrate how the same canons can produce a different treatment for a non-human subject. Their stronger direction used editorial geometry, a panoramic and slightly elevated viewpoint, open landscape, horizontal extent, an industrial band, large sky, warm atmosphere and cool structural shadows. Distance and surrounding space communicated scale.

The weaker directions made the subject into a dramatic poster through an enormous explicit sun, scenic foreground shrubs, unnecessary mountains, ground-level drama or excessive pipe and ladder detail.

This is a lesson about preserving spatial character, not a “refinery at sunset” template for industrial imagery.


# 13. Ethnic Reality, Not Advertising Aspiration

Human representation throughout IELTS Workshops should reflect plausible human and ethnic reality rather than the demographic biases of advertising, stock photography, tourism marketing or aspirational lifestyle imagery.

The core principle is:

> **REPRESENT ETHNIC REALITY, NOT ADVERTISING ASPIRATION.**

Do not allow “professional”, “international”, “modern”, “educated”, “middle class”, “university”, “business”, “European”, “American”, “Omani” or “Gulf” to become visual shorthand for predominantly light-skinned or white people.

Advertising and generated imagery can systematically drift towards lighter skin, conventionally photogenic subjects, affluent-looking people, homogeneous casts and idealised consumer demographics. The IELTS materials should actively resist this tendency.

The goal is not to assign exact ethnic identities based on appearance. Avoid stereotyped visual representations of nationalities when observable contextual characteristics can be specified instead.


## 13.1 Oman and the Gulf

Oman is ethnically and visually diverse. This is particularly important when depicting Dhofar and Oman's coastal communities.

Across the visual system, naturally include people with:

- dark-brown skin;
- Black or Afro-descended appearance;
- medium-brown skin;
- lighter Arab skin tones;
- other appearances plausible within Oman and its international population.

Black and dark-skinned Omanis must appear as ordinary participants in education, work, family life, professional settings, technology, leisure, public life, study and everyday situations.

Do not confine darker-skinned people to particular occupations, socioeconomic roles, traditional settings, background positions or symbolic “diversity” roles. Do not make one darker-skinned person the diversity representative within an otherwise homogenised cast.

Cultural appropriacy concerns behaviour, relationships, clothing and context. It does not mean making everybody ethnically alike.


## 13.2 Global Contexts

Apply the same principle when depicting places outside Oman.

Do not automatically depict Europe, Britain or North America as overwhelmingly white. Do not depict universities, professional workplaces, affluent people or educated people as predominantly white by default.

Contemporary societies and international educational environments contain people from many ethnic backgrounds. Across the material set, naturally include people who may appear Black or Afro-descended, Arab, South Asian, East or Southeast Asian, mixed-heritage, white, and from other backgrounds appropriate to the context.

Do not turn this into a visible diversity checklist. The aim is a believable human population across the materials.


# 14. Representation Without Tokenism

People should be allowed to perform ordinary roles without their ethnicity becoming the subject of the illustration.

For example, a person may simply be:

- studying;
- thinking;
- writing;
- talking;
- working;
- travelling;
- asking for information;
- solving a problem;
- meeting a neighbour;
- using technology.

Do not require cultural explanation for non-white characters.

Do not treat one white/Western character as normal and other characters as visible diversity additions.

Representation should come from observation of the learners' real social environment rather than a generic global diversity template.


## 14.1 Representation Across a Series

Do not require every individual image to contain multiple ethnic groups. A single-person illustration may simply depict one person; a same-gender pair may plausibly share a similar background; and a family may naturally share physical characteristics.

Evaluate representation across the visual system rather than forcing it into every composition.

Ask:

> **If I looked at twenty human illustrations from this course, what kind of world would they suggest exists?**

The answer should not be “a predominantly white or light-skinned world with occasional diversity”. Nor should every image look like a deliberately assembled diversity advertisement. The population should feel natural, varied and unremarkable.


## 14.2 People, Not Demographic Symbols

Ethnicity should normally be incidental to the learning situation unless ethnicity itself is relevant to the lesson.

A Black student is a student. A dark-skinned Omani manager is a manager. A South Asian engineer is an engineer. A white parent is a parent.

Do not visually explain or justify why a person of a particular ethnicity is present. Do not use stereotypical accessories, clothing, architecture or cultural symbols merely to signal ethnicity.

> **REPRESENT ETHNICITY THROUGH PEOPLE, NOT PROPS.**

A Black American student does not need culturally coded accessories. A Black Omani does not need “African” visual cues. An Omani does not need national symbols. A South Asian professional does not need culturally symbolic background decoration. People are allowed simply to exist.


## 14.3 People Need a Reason to Be Together

Ask:

> **WHY ARE THESE PEOPLE TOGETHER?**

Every social grouping should have a visible or contextually plausible reason. Two students may be walking and talking, friends may be studying together, or coworkers may be examining the same material.

Do not distribute unrelated people around an environment merely to make it look lively. Social plausibility matters more than compositional convenience.


# 15. Contemporary Oman Without Cultural Shorthand

The project may feel appropriate to Oman without turning Oman into a visual theme.

The major canon is:

> **CULTURE SHOULD BE INHABITED, NOT ANNOUNCED.**

Cultural plausibility should normally emerge through believable people, relationships, behaviour, ordinary clothing, ordinary activity and context. Cultural symbols added merely to prove a location make a scene less believable.

Explicitly watch for generated imagery inserting the following merely to signal Oman or the Gulf:

- national flags;
- national emblems;
- palm trees;
- mosques;
- minarets;
- generic beige or white “Arabian” buildings;
- mountains;
- frankincense;
- frankincense burners or smoke;
- dhows;
- deserts;
- camels;
- ornamental arches;
- traditional architecture;
- compulsory national dress.

These may appear when genuinely relevant to a task or context.

They are not default identity markers.

A dhow may be necessary in a listening task about traditional fishing; frankincense may be necessary in a lesson about its production; and palms may be necessary in a task about date agriculture. A decorative dhow behind an office worker, frankincense smoke in an ordinary classroom, a palm outside a window solely because the people are Omani, or an emblem behind students is cultural filler.

> **NEVER USE CULTURAL ICONOGRAPHY MERELY AS LOCATION FILLER.**

Contemporary everyday environments are equally important.

Show people studying, working, socialising and participating in ordinary modern life.


## 15.1 The Outsider Test

For any culturally suggestive element, ask:

> **Would this detail probably be here if nobody were trying to communicate the country to an outsider?**

If not, strongly consider removing it.

Apply equivalent comparisons across contexts. British university students do not need Union Jacks, bone-china tea, scones, red telephone boxes or Minis. American students do not need US flags, hamburgers, fries, soda or baseball symbolism. Omani students do not need palms, frankincense, emblems, mosques or stereotypical architecture.

Culture should feel lived rather than labelled.


## 15.2 The Redundancy Test

> **ONCE THE VIEWER UNDERSTANDS SOMETHING, STOP TELLING THEM.**

Do not communicate the same contextual fact repeatedly unless repetition has a genuine compositional purpose. A kumma, Omani emblem, palm, “Arabian” architecture and another palm all announcing “Oman” are redundant. One or two naturally occurring human or contextual signals may already be sufficient.


# 16. Clothing and Omani Representation

Do not make traditional clothing compulsory for characters intended to be plausible in Oman.

An Omani or regionally plausible male character may naturally appear in:

- a dishdasha;
- casual modest clothing;
- a shirt and trousers;
- work clothing;
- other contextually appropriate contemporary clothing.

Traditional clothing should appear where natural rather than functioning as an ethnic label.

Young adults should be allowed to look like young adults.

Professionals do not automatically require suits.


# 17. Modesty in Female Representation

For illustrations representing women in the target Omani learning environment, use a consistently modest visual treatment so that materials remain comfortable and appropriate for the widest range of learners.

As the default:

- hair should be covered;
- chest and shoulders should be covered;
- arms should be covered;
- clothing should be loose or represented through simplified flowing forms rather than emphasising the body;
- poses should be natural, social, academic or professional rather than glamour-oriented.

This does not mean every female character should look identical.

Use variety in:

- colour;
- garment form;
- silhouette;
- styling;
- age;
- pose;
- context.

Vector illustration allows modest clothing and covering to be suggested elegantly through shape and composition without requiring photorealistic detail.

This is a visual-design decision for cultural comfort in the target learning environment. It should not be presented as a statement about women's identity or behaviour.


# 18. Dignity in Human Representation

All people should be represented with dignity.

Avoid idealising bodies or making physical appearance the visual focus.

Characters should primarily be defined by:

- what they are doing;
- what they are thinking about;
- who they are communicating with;
- the situation they are in.

The illustration system should feel observational and human rather than aspirational or promotional.

The broader principle is:

> **OBSERVATION, NOT ADVERTISING.**

People should look occupied rather than posed. Relationships should be socially plausible rather than compositionally convenient. Human appearance should reflect the place rather than advertising conventions.

Avoid prospectus poses, influencer posture, catalogue posture, corporate smiles and excessive attractiveness or polish across the visual system. Do not make everybody look confident, happy and aspirational.

Do not systematically beautify, lighten, homogenise or affluent-code the people represented. Across a series, include ordinary variation in skin tone, facial features, body shape, age, hair, clothing and posture, as well as concentration, listening, thinking, uncertainty, disagreement, fatigue and neutral expressions where contextually plausible.

A pleasant or attractive person is not a problem. The problem is a whole visual world populated like aspirational advertising.

Avoid influencer aesthetics, catalogue posing and idealised lifestyle imagery.


## 18.1 Human Reference Models

Two recent student illustrations provide useful internal reference models, not templates to copy mechanically.

An illustration of two Black American university students walking and talking works because it uses strong colour, large graphic shapes, clear human presence, expressive gesture, a legible relationship and a simplified subordinate campus. Their mutual orientation, eye contact and open-hand gesture communicate conversation. Ethnic identity is carried naturally by the people rather than props.

An illustration of two young Omani men studying works because one wears a plain red T-shirt while the other naturally wears a dishdasha and kumma; the clothing feels inhabited rather than symbolic and carries no logos. Eye-lines, listening posture and an explanatory hand gesture establish the relationship, while a laptop and study material make the activity legible against a simple background. Future versions should retain this economy: a laptop and one or two notebooks or books are normally sufficient.


# 19. Three Roles for Visuals

Maintain an explicit distinction between three visual roles.


## 19.1 Task Visual

The visual itself contains information the learner must interpret.

Examples include:

- line graph;
- bar chart;
- pie chart;
- table;
- maps;
- process diagram.

Accuracy, legibility and information integrity are paramount. During active IELTS practice, the task visual should normally have priority over contextual or atmospheric imagery.


## 19.2 Context Illustration

The illustration establishes the human, geographical or social world of a task.

Examples include:

- people using public transport;
- adult students on a university campus;
- an adult working from home;
- neighbours in a residential environment;
- a property manager speaking with a tenant.

Context illustration must not provide or imply the correct answer. People should look as though they belong in the situation rather than posing for educational software.


## 19.3 Atmospheric / Transition Visual

An occasional visual may establish place, mood or a transition into a substantially different topic or learning phase.

Possible locations include a workshop opening or meaningful major-section transition. Use this role most sparingly. It may be visually expressive but must not interfere with learning or become routine decoration.

Do not confuse these roles. Instructional diagrams such as relationship maps, grouping diagrams or **Focus → Develop → Close** may still clarify a concept, but they are information graphics within the learning interface rather than context illustration.


## 19.4 Listening Visual Information Leakage

Images used in Listening have an additional pedagogical constraint:

> **THE IMAGE CAN GIVE INFORMATION BEFORE THE AUDIO DOES.**

Every Listening visual must therefore be checked for visual information leakage. Ask:

> **What information is the image allowed to give away?**

A visual may establish context already supplied by the task, orient the learner, distinguish speakers, support navigation or reduce irrelevant cognitive load.

It must not accidentally reveal an answer, a distinction learners are supposed to hear, a key inference, a sequence they must identify, vocabulary whose recognition is being tested, or the solution to a matching or classification task.

A refinery image may be appropriate when the task already establishes that the listening concerns petroleum or refining. The same image may be inappropriate when recognising the industry is something learners must infer from the audio.

> **VISUAL QUALITY AND PEDAGOGICAL SUITABILITY ARE SEPARATE TESTS.**

A beautiful image can still be the wrong image for a Listening task. This global canon should be expanded later in Listening-specific documentation.


# 20. Interface Philosophy

The interface should feel like a learning workspace.

Avoid turning every piece of content into a separate card.

Use:

- typography;
- spacing;
- alignment;
- background changes;
- restrained dividers;
- grouping;
- selective panels

before introducing additional containers.

Cards and panels should exist when they communicate a meaningful functional boundary.


# 21. Primary Desktop Workspace

Laptop and desktop are the primary environments for serious production tasks.

Where useful, use workspace layouts such as:

**SOURCE / TASK | LEARNER WORKSPACE**

Examples:

Academic Writing:

**graph or task | writing area**

General Writing:

**situation and bullet points | writing area**

Future Task 2:

**question / planning | response**

The learner should be able to consult the task while writing without excessive navigation.

Take advantage of desktop width rather than stretching a narrow mobile layout across the screen.


# 22. Smartphone Principle

Smartphone access is a first-class review and practice experience, not an afterthought.

However, the phone does not need to imitate the desktop IELTS test interface.

Mobile is particularly suitable for:

- review;
- self-study;
- model investigation;
- language practice;
- short writing activities;
- revisiting feedback;
- vocabulary/language support.

Serious timed writing practice is primarily intended for a laptop or desktop.


# 23. Responsive Behaviour

Desktop columns should not simply shrink until they become unreadable.

On narrow screens, restructure the information.

For example:

Desktop:

**TASK | WRITING**

Mobile:

**TASK  
↓  
WRITING  
↓  
SUPPORT**

Task information should remain easy to reopen or consult while writing.

Possible mechanisms may include:

- collapsible task panel;
- sticky task button;
- compact task summary.

The final behaviour should be tested during prototyping.

Illustrations must follow the same responsive philosophy. On desktop, a context illustration may sit beside introductory or task context, or occasionally occupy a restrained wide editorial panel. On mobile, it should stack naturally, preserve important content and avoid excessive vertical dominance. A learner must never have to scroll through a huge decorative image before reaching the task.

Illustration remains secondary to the learning interaction.


# 24. Touch and Interaction

No essential information or function may depend exclusively on hover.

Any hover interaction used on desktop must have a touch-accessible equivalent.

Interactive targets should be comfortably usable with fingers.

Drag-and-drop should have a tap/select alternative where practical.

Do not require precision gestures that are difficult on a phone.


# 25. Writing Workspace

Writing areas should feel substantial enough for genuine composition.

Prioritise:

- comfortable width;
- readable font size;
- generous line height;
- clear paragraph breaks;
- visible word count where pedagogically appropriate;
- reliable text persistence;
- easy editing.

Support controls should not crowd the writing area.

The learner's writing should remain visually more important than surrounding interface controls.


# 26. Help and Progressive Disclosure

Hints, language support and models should normally remain secondary until requested.

Do not display all available help simultaneously.

Use progressive disclosure so the learner can attempt the task before receiving additional support.

Help should be visually easy to find but should not dominate the initial screen.


# 27. Feedback States

Feedback should be clear without becoming visually aggressive.

Do not rely solely on red/green distinctions.

Use combinations of:

- wording;
- icons where useful;
- borders;
- subtle colour;
- position;
- explanatory text.

Feedback styling should support reflection and another attempt rather than imitate a game-show correct/incorrect response.


# 28. Motion and Animation

Motion should be restrained and purposeful.

Possible uses include:

- revealing support;
- showing a relationship;
- demonstrating grouping;
- transitioning between related stages;
- showing before/after change.

Avoid animation merely to make the site feel modern.

Do not use constant movement, bouncing controls or distracting entrance effects.

The learner should be able to concentrate on text and information without visual competition.


# 29. Charts, Maps and Information Graphics

Academic task visuals must prioritise accuracy and readability.

They should be:

- clean;
- clearly labelled;
- sufficiently large;
- easy to interpret;
- visually consistent with the platform without becoming decorative.

Do not simplify a task visual in a way that changes the information the learner is expected to interpret.

Colour in charts should remain distinguishable and accessible.

Data visualisation should support the data-literacy principle:

> What is the information telling me?


# 30. Visual Consistency Across Workshops

Writing, Listening, Reading and future workshops should clearly belong to the same platform.

Share:

- typography;
- spacing logic;
- interaction conventions;
- editorial illustration canons rather than a rigid rendering formula;
- core interface components;
- overall colour philosophy.

Individual skills may use subtle secondary visual identities or accent families.

Do not make each workshop look like a separate unrelated website. Do not force all subjects into one house cartoon style merely to demonstrate consistency.


# 31. Skill-Specific Visual Identity

The subject matter itself should provide much of each workshop's visual character.

For Writing, visual emphasis may come from:

- writing spaces;
- charts;
- maps;
- processes;
- writer/reader relationships;
- paragraph architecture.

For Listening, it may later come from:

- audio;
- speakers;
- listening contexts;
- sequencing;
- note-taking.

For Reading, it may later come from:

- text;
- highlighting;
- information relationships;
- navigation through passages.

Do not force identical visual compositions onto different skills.


# 32. Accessibility and Legibility

Visual elegance must not reduce usability.

Prioritise:

- sufficient text contrast;
- readable font sizes;
- keyboard accessibility where practical;
- visible focus states;
- touch-friendly controls;
- meaningful labels;
- information that does not depend solely on colour;
- responsive layouts;
- predictable interaction behaviour.

Accessibility should be built into the design rather than added after completion.

Relevant illustrations require useful alternative text that describes meaningful contextual content without interpreting an argument for the learner. Purely atmospheric or decorative visuals should not create unnecessary screen-reader noise. Essential task instructions must never exist only in an image.

Colour-zone distinctions must always be reinforced by textual or structural signals. All colour families and their active states require sufficient contrast.


# 33. What the System Should NOT Feel Like

IELTS Workshops should not feel like:

- a photocopied worksheet placed online;
- Moodle;
- a children's learning application;
- a corporate HR training portal;
- a generic AI-generated landing page;
- a dashboard full of cards;
- an exam-preparation book broken into web pages;
- a tourism advertisement for Oman;
- a Western ESL product with token diversity inserted.


# 34. What the System SHOULD Feel Like

The intended experience is:

> **A calm, contemporary, intelligent learning workspace designed for adults.**

It should feel visually considered without demanding attention for its design.

It should be locally credible without becoming culturally performative.

It should be diverse without advertising diversity.

It should be professional without becoming corporate.

It should be educational without looking like school material.

It should be visually interesting without becoming visually busy.


# 35. Design Test

Before approving a screen, ask:

1. What job is this visual doing?
2. What is the learner supposed to focus on?
3. Is that visually dominant?
4. Is anything present merely to fill space?
5. Could anything be removed?
6. Does colour communicate something useful?
7. Is an illustration establishing context or deliberately creating atmosphere?
8. Does the screen feel appropriate for an adult?
9. Does human representation feel plausible and respectful in the target environment?
10. Does the interface look generic because familiar web components have been used without thought?
11. Does the design work on both a laptop and a narrow phone screen?
12. Is every essential interaction available without hover?
13. Does the screen have enough space to breathe?
14. Is the design supporting the pedagogy rather than competing with it?

A visual should normally have a clear job such as distinguishing a learning phase, providing task information, establishing human or geographical context, or marking a meaningful transition. If its only job is to “make the page more interesting”, that is usually insufficient.

If visual simplification improves the learning experience, simplify.

For every significant illustration, make this a primary question:

> **WHY AM I LOOKING AT THIS?**

Every significant element needs a defensible answer. “It establishes study”, “it establishes their relationship”, “it makes the gesture legible”, “it establishes university with minimal information”, “it creates necessary visual balance” and “it is the subject of the learning task” are useful answers.

“It makes the room look lived-in”, “it makes the image more Omani”, “it fills an empty wall”, “it makes the image more interesting”, “it makes the image feel realistic” and “it normally appears in AI images of this place” are weak answers. Remove or simplify the element.

Use the complete editorial-illustration review:

1. What is the one main visual idea?
2. Why am I looking at this?
3. Why is this person here?
4. What are they actually doing?
5. Would the scene remain plausible if the people did not know a camera was there?
6. Why are these people together?
7. What is the visual focus?
8. Does every important object have a job?
9. Is the background providing context or inventing a world?
10. Is culture being inhabited or announced?
11. Would this cultural detail exist if nobody were trying to signal the country?
12. Has the image drifted towards advertising aspiration?
13. Does ethnic representation feel ordinary rather than symbolic?
14. Across the course, who is being allowed to look educated, professional, intelligent, ordinary, affluent, contemporary, technically skilled and academically capable?
15. Are there more objects than the action requires?
16. Does gesture do enough of the communicative work?
17. Is the crop confident?
18. Does colour contribute to the composition?
19. If the image were blurred, would its large-scale composition still work?
20. Has simplification preserved the subject's spatial character?
21. Is the same information being communicated redundantly?
22. If this is a Listening visual, what information does it give the learner before the audio?
23. Does it accidentally reveal something the learner is supposed to hear or infer?

If these questions reveal unnecessary information, weak composition, cultural signalling, advertising bias, demographic homogenisation, tokenism, visual leakage or redundant detail, revise before accepting the image.


# 36. Task 2 Context and Argumentative Neutrality

Task 2 may particularly benefit from context illustration because its questions concern people, education, work, cities, transport, technology, environment, families, society, public policy and everyday decisions.

For example, an education question may begin with adult students in a contemporary university environment; a working-from-home question may show an adult working naturally in an ordinary domestic setting; and an urban-transport question may show pedestrians, buses, cars and everyday city activity.

These visuals establish the world of the question. They must not tell the learner which position to take, which advantage is strongest, which solution is correct or what opinion the workshop prefers.

Before using an illustration for a contested question, ask:

> **Could this image make one answer, position or argument appear obviously preferable?**

If yes, revise or remove it. Do not depict one side through pollution, congestion or unhappy people while depicting another through sunshine, nature or happiness unless the task itself explicitly supplies that information. Context illustration must maintain argumentative neutrality.


# 37. Location and Cultural Context in Use

The primary audience currently includes adult IELTS learners in Oman. Where contextually appropriate, illustrations may naturally reflect Oman, the Gulf, contemporary Arab environments and the international population learners plausibly encounter.

Do not force Oman into every illustration or turn ordinary learning content into tourism imagery. Use plausible contemporary architecture and environments, adults engaged in ordinary activities, contextually appropriate modest clothing, the established modest treatment for women in the target Omani environment, and natural variation in male clothing according to context.

Avoid obligatory mosque silhouettes, camels, national flags, stereotyped desert imagery and token cultural props. Diversity should emerge naturally from the situation rather than appearing as a visibly assembled representation exercise.


# 38. Adoption and Existing Work

These principles apply permanently across Writing, Reading, Listening, Speaking and all future IELTS materials.

The two successful student illustrations described in Section 18.1 are useful internal reference models for future visual judgement, not mandatory templates. The refinery experiment is evidence that the same canons can produce a distinct non-human treatment while preserving subject-specific spatial character.

Do not weaken the representation safeguards in future visual work. Continue to evaluate the complete visual system for who is allowed to appear educated, professional, intelligent, ordinary, affluent, contemporary, technically skilled and academically capable. International contexts must not drift towards white-majority advertising imagery merely because generated media commonly does so. Representation should feel normal rather than assembled.

Do not bulk-retrofit existing workshops merely to demonstrate the updated system. Change existing images when they are actively being rebuilt, clearly violate the current principles or are selected for a deliberate visual refresh. Do not generate illustration assets until a specific learning context and visual job have been defined.
