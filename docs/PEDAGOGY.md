# IELTS Workshops — Pedagogy Specification

**Version:** 1.1  
**Status:** Foundation specification

## 1. Purpose

IELTS Workshops is a computer-first learning system for IELTS preparation.

It is designed primarily for serious learning and production on a laptop or desktop computer. It must also work effectively on smartphones, particularly for review, self-study, short practice, language support and revisiting previous learning.

The platform must not simply reproduce printed worksheets on a screen.

Its purpose is to use the digital environment where this creates genuine learning value: helping learners notice, decide, practise, receive appropriate support, compare, diagnose, revise and gradually become more independent.

The platform may eventually contain Writing, Listening, Reading, Speaking and other IELTS preparation modules.

Each skill requires its own pedagogy. Platform-wide principles should be shared where appropriate, but a successful interaction in Writing must not automatically be copied into Listening, Reading or Speaking.


# 2. Core Learning Cycle

The preferred learning cycle is:

**NOTICE → DECIDE → ATTEMPT → COMPARE → REVISE**

The learner should normally do something before being shown an answer or complete model.

Avoid making the dominant experience:

**EXPLAIN → SHOW MODEL → COPY → MOVE ON**

Explanation and models are valuable, but they should support learner thinking rather than replace it.


# 3. Thinking Before Terminology

Where possible, learners should encounter and understand the underlying problem before technical terminology is introduced.

For example, before defining an Academic Writing Task 1 overview, ask:

> What do you notice when you look at the whole graph?

Terminology should clarify an idea the learner is beginning to understand, rather than become another concept to memorise.


# 4. Separate Thinking Problems from Language Problems

A learner may fail because they do not know **what to say**, or because they know what they want to say but do not know **how to express it**.

These are different learning problems.

The platform should distinguish, where useful, between:

**COGNITIVE / TASK PROBLEM**
- I do not understand the information.
- I do not know what is important.
- I cannot decide what belongs together.
- I do not understand what the question requires.

and:

**LANGUAGE PROBLEM**
- I know what I mean but cannot express it.
- I need a useful phrase or structure.
- My sentence contains errors.
- I want to express the idea more precisely.

Do not automatically solve a thinking problem by giving the learner more vocabulary.


# 5. Teach the Skill, Not the Interaction

Interactions must have a pedagogical purpose.

Do not add multiple choice, matching, drag-and-drop, animation, scoring or gamification merely to make a page appear interactive.

Before adding an interaction, ask:

> What ability is the learner practising by doing this?

Useful interactions may develop noticing, selecting, categorising, comparing, predicting, organising, constructing, judging, editing or revising.

If clicking the correct answer teaches very little, redesign the activity.


# 6. Accessible Solutions First

The platform should make successful performance feel achievable.

Do not imply that difficult vocabulary, long sentences or sophisticated-looking structures are necessary for good IELTS performance.

Where a clear, natural and controllable solution exists, learners should encounter it.

More flexible or sophisticated language can then expand the learner's repertoire.

The system should reduce unnecessary cognitive load rather than manufacture difficulty.


# 7. Authority and IELTS Claims

The platform must distinguish official IELTS requirements from teaching recommendations.

Use three levels:

### IELTS requires

Use only for requirements supported by official IELTS information or assessment criteria.

### We recommend

Use for dependable strategies that help learners perform the task but are not IELTS rules.

### Useful option

Use for techniques, expressions or approaches that may help but are not necessary.

Never convert a teaching preference into an IELTS requirement.

For example:

**Incorrect:**  
IELTS Task 1 must contain two detail paragraphs.

**Correct:**  
We recommend two detail paragraphs because this usually makes it easier to group related information and avoid an overloaded paragraph.

Avoid unsupported claims such as:

- an overview must never contain numbers;
- every paragraph must contain a fixed number of sentences;
- particular reporting verbs correspond to particular bands;
- every word in a question must be paraphrased;
- complicated language automatically produces a higher score.


# 8. Scaffolding and Progressive Independence

Learners require different amounts of support.

Do not create completely separate courses for assumed band levels unless there is a strong pedagogical reason.

Prefer graduated support within activities.

Possible controls include:

**More support | Standard | Challenge me**

These labels are examples, not mandatory interface wording.

Support should reduce as learners become more capable.

A sequence may move from:

**SELECT → CONSTRUCT → WRITE WITH SUPPORT → WRITE INDEPENDENTLY**

The final goal is performance with minimal support under conditions increasingly similar to the real task.

A learner should not become better at using the course while remaining dependent on the course.


# 9. Clear → Expand → Explore

Where useful, language support may present several legitimate possibilities.

### CLEAR

Natural language the learner can probably control comfortably.

### EXPAND

Another useful way to communicate the same idea or slightly richer meaning.

### EXPLORE

Language or structures the learner may want to investigate or experiment with.

These labels do **not** mean:

**weak → good → best**

All three may be effective.

More sophisticated language is not automatically better.

Useful questions include:

> Which sentence would you feel most comfortable writing?

and:

> Why did you choose it?

A learner may successfully write **Clear** while learning something from **Explore**.

**Clear → Expand → Explore is a support technique, not a mandatory component of every activity.**


# 10. Models

Models are learning objects, not scripts to memorise.

Where useful, models should make the writer's thinking visible.

They may show:

- what the writer noticed;
- what was selected;
- what was ignored;
- how information was organised;
- why a particular expression works;
- what makes the response clear;
- what limits a nearly successful response.

Prefer investigated or annotated models to unexplained model answers.

Where appropriate, learners may interact with models through highlighting, annotations or comparison.

Do not assign invented IELTS band scores to internally produced teaching examples.

Use labels such as:

- teaching example;
- developing response;
- stronger response;
- likely characteristics;
- examiner lens.

Only genuine official samples should be presented as official band examples.


# 11. Feedback Must Lead Somewhere

Feedback should normally create another learner action.

Preferred loop:

**ATTEMPT → FEEDBACK → UNDERSTAND → REVISE → COMPARE**

Where possible, learners should improve their original response rather than simply reveal and copy an answer.


# 12. Diagnostic Help

A generic **Need some help?** control should not automatically reveal the solution.

Where appropriate, help should distinguish between problems such as:

**I don't know WHAT to say.**  
Support noticing, interpretation, selection or planning.

**I know WHAT I want to say, but not HOW.**  
Provide relevant language support or models.

**I tried, but I think there are ERRORS.**  
Provide targeted accuracy support.

**I can say it, but I want to say it BETTER.**  
Offer selective language expansion.

The exact wording and options should depend on the skill and activity.


# 13. Classroom and Self-Study

Classroom and self-study should form two routes through the same learning system.

The classroom route should be focused and manageable.

Self-study can provide:

- additional examples;
- repeated practice;
- language expansion;
- targeted micro-skills;
- model investigation;
- revision opportunities;
- independent performance.

Do not require every learner to complete every available activity during class.

The platform may be deep while the classroom path remains short.


# 14. Self-Study Improvement Loop

Self-study should not simply mean more exercises.

Preferred cycle:

**NOTICE A WEAKNESS  
→ IDENTIFY THE TYPE OF PROBLEM  
→ GET APPROPRIATE HELP  
→ PRACTISE THE SPECIFIC SKILL  
→ RETURN TO WRITING/TASK  
→ REVISE  
→ TRY A LATER TASK MORE INDEPENDENTLY**

Self-study should progressively reduce dependence rather than provide permanent scaffolding.


# 15. AI Support

AI support is optional and does not need to be integrated directly into the website.

Where useful, provide mechanisms such as:

**Copy my answer**

and:

**Copy for AI feedback**

An AI-feedback package should provide enough context for an external AI system to understand the task and learner response.

Prompts should normally ask AI to:

1. diagnose;
2. explain briefly;
3. identify a small number of useful improvements;
4. ask the learner to revise.

Avoid making the primary AI workflow:

> Rewrite this as Band 8.

AI should support another learner attempt rather than replace it.


# 16. Computer-Based Performance

Computer performance is a training strand, not an IELTS assessment criterion.

Learners should progressively practise:

- typing;
- editing;
- paragraph breaks;
- punctuation;
- capitalisation;
- apostrophes;
- moving between task information and answer;
- monitoring word count;
- time management;
- efficient revision.

When adapting older paper activities, ask:

> Would I still teach this if every learner were sitting at an IELTS computer?

Paper-based actions should not survive merely because they appeared in an older worksheet.


# 17. Writing — Common Principles

Writing instruction should broadly prioritise:

**THINKING → ORGANISATION → COMMUNICATION → LANGUAGE REFINEMENT**

rather than:

**ADVANCED VOCABULARY → FORMULA → MODEL IMITATION**

The four IELTS Writing assessment criteria remain primary:

- Task Achievement / Task Response
- Coherence and Cohesion
- Lexical Resource
- Grammatical Range and Accuracy

Memory tools used in the course are teaching devices, not official IELTS terminology.

Current teaching memory tools:

**Academic Task 1:**  
IOC = Important information · Overview · Comparisons

**General Task 1:**  
BTP = Bullet points · Tone · Purpose

**Task 2:**  
AES = Answer everything · Explain · Support


# 18. Academic Writing Task 1

Academic Task 1 contains an important underlying information-literacy skill:

> **What is the information telling me?**

Learners should not merely convert individual numbers or visual elements into sentences.

They should learn to:

- interpret;
- select;
- identify patterns;
- identify meaningful relationships;
- group information;
- compare;
- support observations with appropriate evidence;
- communicate the result logically.

This capability is useful beyond IELTS and should be reinforced throughout Academic Task 1.


## 18.1 Recognising the Visual

Learners should recognise common visual forms accurately:

- line graph;
- bar chart;
- pie chart;
- table;
- map;
- process;
- combinations.

For teaching purposes they may also be grouped as:

**DATA**  
line / bar / pie / table / combinations

**CHANGE IN PLACE**  
maps

**SEQUENCE**  
processes

These are visual types, not completely separate writing systems.


## 18.2 Introduction

Core principle:

> **MEANING FIRST. CHANGE SECOND.**

Learners identify what the visual actually shows before attempting to reformulate the task.

A dependable introduction normally communicates:

- what is shown;
- what is measured or described;
- where or whom, where relevant;
- when, where relevant.

One clear sentence will often be sufficient.

Do not require learners to replace every word from the task.

Keep precise technical nouns where replacing them would make the sentence less natural or accurate.

Prefer safe reformulation through:

- natural structural change;
- word-class change;
- selective synonymy;
- reordering information;
- retaining important nouns.

A good paraphrase preserves meaning.


## 18.3 Overview

Core question:

> **What do you notice when you look at the WHOLE visual?**

Recommended process:

**READ → LOOK → NOTICE → SAY IT**

**READ**  
Understand the title, question, labels, key, units and dates.

**LOOK**  
Step back from individual details.

**NOTICE**  
Identify the major pattern, contrast, transformation or journey.

**SAY IT**  
Communicate the big picture clearly.

Recommended easy solution:

> Start with **Overall,**

This is a teaching recommendation, not an IELTS requirement.

For **data**, useful questions include:

- What stands out?
- What generally rises or falls?
- What is significantly higher or lower?
- What relationships are worth mentioning?

For **maps**:

- What was the place like before?
- What is it like afterwards?
- What are the biggest changes?

For **processes**:

- Where does it start?
- Where does it finish?
- Does it finish or return to the beginning?
- What broadly happens between these points?

The overview normally does not need exact figures because its main job is to communicate the big picture.

Do not teach this as an absolute prohibition on numbers.


## 18.4 Detail Paragraphs

IELTS does not require exactly two detail paragraphs.

The course recommends **two** because this usually helps learners:

- group related information;
- make useful comparisons;
- prevent overloaded paragraphs;
- create a clear structure.

Core thinking process:

**UNDERSTAND → GROUP → COMPARE → SUPPORT → WRITE**

**UNDERSTAND**  
What is the information telling me?

**GROUP**  
Which information belongs together?

**COMPARE**  
What meaningful relationships exist?

**SUPPORT**  
Which figures or details demonstrate those relationships?

**WRITE**  
Communicate the group clearly.


### Grouping

Do not automatically group according to visual position.

Avoid teaching:

> Describe the left half in Paragraph 1 and the right half in Paragraph 2.

Prefer:

> Put information together when there is a useful relationship between it.

Possible groupings include:

- higher versus lower values;
- similar trends;
- contrasting trends;
- strong changes versus stable categories;
- connected map changes;
- early versus later process stages.

There may be more than one rational organisation.


### Comparison

Comparison should be meaningful before it is linguistically sophisticated.

A simple comparison can be useful:

> A was higher than B.

Greater language control may later express richer relationships:

> A remained considerably higher than B throughout the period.

Teach learners to notice **why** information is worth comparing.


### Paragraph Architecture

Use:

**FOCUS → DEVELOP → CLOSE**

**FOCUS**  
What is this paragraph mainly showing?

**DEVELOP**  
Give relevant evidence, details and comparisons.

For Academic Task 1, development means reporting and organising what the visual shows. Do not invent explanations for why the data changed unless that information is provided.

**CLOSE**  
What is the last useful thing to say about this group?

Then stop.

Closure does not mean adding a compulsory mini-conclusion.

A final comparison or piece of evidence may already complete the paragraph.

Useful guidance:

> **Close the idea. Don't add a conclusion.**


# 19. General Training Writing Task 1

General Task 1 contains a different underlying communication skill.

Core questions:

> **WHO am I writing to?**

> **WHY am I writing?**

> **WHAT does this person need from me?**

The learner should understand the reader, relationship and purpose before choosing language.


## 19.1 Planning

Use:

**WHO → WHY → WHAT**

**WHO**  
Who is the reader and what is my relationship with them?

**WHY**  
Why am I writing?

**WHAT**  
What information or action is required by the bullet points?

BTP — Bullet points, Tone, Purpose — may be used as a memory/checking tool.


## 19.2 Tone

Do not reduce tone to two vocabulary lists labelled FORMAL and INFORMAL.

Tone depends on:

- reader;
- relationship;
- purpose;
- situation.

Learners should judge language in context.

Language appropriate to a close friend, new neighbour, colleague, landlord, manager or organisation may differ.

Teach language through communicative choices rather than isolated formality labels.


## 19.3 Opening

The opening should quickly establish the communicative purpose.

Core question:

> **Why is this person receiving my letter?**

Provide accessible natural options through Clear → Expand → Explore where this is useful.


## 19.4 Development

All required bullet points must be addressed.

Do not teach learners merely to mention each bullet point.

Core principle:

> **Give the reader enough information to understand and respond.**

Related information should be organised together.

Do not teach:

> One bullet point = one paragraph

as an IELTS rule.

A paragraph should have a clear communicative job.


## 19.5 Closing

The ending should complete the communication naturally.

Core question:

> **What does the reader need at the end?**

The appropriate closing depends on the communicative purpose.

It may involve:

- requesting action;
- confirming an invitation;
- apologising;
- expressing hope;
- requesting further information;
- indicating an expected response.

Core principle:

> **Close the communication. Don't just stop writing.**


## 19.6 General Task 1 Self-Study

Self-study should provide multiple communicative situations.

Examples may include:

- new neighbour;
- friend;
- colleague;
- landlord;
- hotel manager;
- course administrator;
- organisation.

The same writing workspace may load different IELTS-style tasks.

Each situation should provide optional **context-specific language support**.

Do not provide one enormous generic phrase bank.

Instead, provide natural language useful for the particular relationship and communicative purpose.

Where useful, allow learners to select a small number of phrases to keep beside the writing area while composing.

The goal is to build a flexible repertoire through repeated communicative encounters.


# 20. Writing Task 2

Task 2 follows one connected learning journey:

**UNDERSTAND THE QUESTION → BUILD YOUR ANSWER → WRITE THE ESSAY → CHECK & IMPROVE → TRANSFER / INDEPENDENT PRACTICE**

Its classroom core is designed for approximately four hours. Extended self-study may continue for as long as the learner finds it useful. These are not separate courses: they are two routes through the same pedagogy.

The classroom route keeps the central journey focused and manageable. It prioritises exact question understanding, Task Response, relevant ideas, explanation and support, reasoning relationships, paragraph development, organisation, genuine writing, diagnosis, revision and transfer. It should expose useful language discoveries without trying to teach every grammar, vocabulary or style point explicitly.

Extended self-study grows from learning moments encountered on that journey. It offers short, focused investigations that learners can enter, leave and revisit without completing a second linear syllabus. Every investigation should feed back into the learner's own writing.


## 20.1 Task Response as the spine

Task Response remains the main organising logic of Task 2.

Core distinction:

> **GOOD ENGLISH ≠ GOOD TASK RESPONSE.**

A sentence can be grammatical, broadly relevant and reasonably academic while contributing little to the exact answer.

Teach learners to distinguish:

**ABOUT THE TOPIC**

from:

**HELPS ANSWER THE QUESTION**

Before writing, learners should identify:

**TOPIC**  
What is this about?

**TASK**  
What exactly am I being asked to do?

**LIMITS**  
What specific aspect, population, situation or scope must I address?

**Topic → Task → Limits** is an optional workshop thinking tool, not official IELTS terminology. Task types remain useful when they help learners identify the required actions.

Instruction words should be treated as actions, not merely essay labels.

Examples:

**To what extent do you agree or disagree?**  
→ decide and support a position.

**Discuss both views and give your own opinion.**  
→ explain both positions and make your own position clear.

**What are the causes and what solutions can you suggest?**  
→ explain causes and propose relevant solutions.

**Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?**  
→ consider both and make a judgement.

Do not reduce Task Response to a task-type label. Instruction words describe actions the response must perform.


## 20.2 Sophisticated thinking, accessible language

Use this permanent principle:

> **SOPHISTICATED THINKING, ACCESSIBLE LANGUAGE.**

Learners may make difficult judgements about relevance, meaning, comparison, reasoning and subtle differences. Learner-facing instructions should nevertheless normally be manageable for a developing A2–B1 learner.

> **DIFFICULT THINKING ≠ DIFFICULT ENGLISH.**

Do not make the explanation of a skill harder than the skill itself. A learner should not lose a judgement task merely because one option is unnecessarily difficult to decode. This applies particularly to distractors.

Most teaching examples should use vocabulary and syntax an A2–B1 learner can understand with little or modest support. Stronger control may still be modelled, but avoid needlessly dense abstract nouns, rare vocabulary, long noun phrases, nested clauses and unexplained terminology.

Keep genuinely useful language and make it accessible through optional meanings, simpler explanations, short examples and context-specific support. Help is a normal part of the environment, not a remedial track; use neutral labels such as **Need help?**, **What does this mean?**, **Look closer**, **Another example** and **More support**.


## 20.3 The official criteria in experience

The four official IELTS Writing criteria remain primary and must not be replaced with workshop terminology:

- **Task Response:** Am I actually answering what I was asked?
- **Coherence and Cohesion:** Can the reader follow how one idea leads to the next?
- **Lexical Resource:** Are my words expressing what I mean clearly, naturally and precisely?
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy:** Can I choose different sentence structures to express relationships clearly and control them accurately?

These questions are learner-facing explanations, not replacements for official band-descriptor wording. Do not make unsupported band-score claims.

Task Response provides the main spine. Coherence and Cohesion becomes concrete through progression, paragraph focus, development, controlled reference and clear relationships—not connector count. Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range and Accuracy grow from communicating an answer more clearly, naturally and precisely.


## 20.4 Teach language through communicative purpose

Ask:

> **WHAT DOES THIS LANGUAGE LET THE WRITER COMMUNICATE MORE CLEARLY, PRECISELY OR EFFICIENTLY?**

Do not teach grammar or vocabulary as feature collection. Avoid claims that passive voice is more academic, complex sentences score more points or a particular form is required for a band.

Use the permanent applied-grammar question:

> **WHAT DOES THIS STRUCTURE HELP ME DO?**

Use **EXPLAIN FIRST, LABEL SECOND**. Learners may encounter useful terminology after understanding the communicative job; terminology is not the entry requirement.

Foreground cross-skill thinking relationships:

- contrast;
- concession;
- reason / cause;
- result / consequence;
- condition;
- comparison;
- certainty / qualification.

Learners often notice these relationships in Reading and Listening and create them in Speaking and Writing. This is a cross-skill cognitive framework, not an IELTS trick.

Give contrast and concession particular attention through the thinking pattern:

**THIS IS TRUE → BUT → THIS OTHER POINT STILL MATTERS**

Use accessible examples with *although*, *while*, *but* and *however*, while making clear that sentence-level grammar and sentence-to-sentence linking are not interchangeable formulae.

Teach reason, result and condition as ways to make reasoning explicit. Accessible language may include *because*, *since*, *so*, *therefore*, *which can lead to* and *if*. Do not turn these into connector lists.

Teach sentence range as choice:

> **RANGE MEANS HAVING CHOICES.**

A simple sentence may be best. A complex sentence can express a useful relationship, but complexity without control can reduce clarity and accuracy. Longer is not inherently better.

Teach active and passive voice as **control of focus**. Use passive voice when the action, process or result deserves more attention than the actor; use active voice when the actor matters or it is clearer. Never teach **passive = more academic = better IELTS**.

Teach qualification as accurate thinking. More certainty is not more academic. Language such as *may*, *can*, *often*, *sometimes*, *tends to*, *for some people* and *in some cases* can prevent unsupported absolute claims.

Teach comparison for argumentative purpose: it can show why one option matters, why a consequence is stronger, why groups experience something differently or why one solution may be preferable.


## 20.5 Register, pronouns and lexical choice

Teach:

> **TASK 2 IS AN ESSAY, NOT A CONVERSATION WITH THE READER.**

Direct advice with *you* may be grammatical but create an unnecessarily conversational writer–reader relationship. Explain this relationship accessibly before using abstract terminology such as *general claim*.

Do not teach a blanket ban on pronouns. *I believe* can be acceptable when a task requests a personal position. Direct reader-address with *you* is often the more important issue.

Register is not maximum formality. Use a continuum where useful:

**TOO CONVERSATIONAL ← APPROPRIATE → UNNECESSARILY FORMAL / COMPLICATED**

Academic writing should still sound like natural English.

For Lexical Resource, use:

> **PRECISION BEFORE SOPHISTICATION.**

The best word expresses the intended meaning clearly, naturally and precisely; it is not necessarily the most difficult word the learner knows. Teach useful combinations in arguments—such as *increase access*, *address a problem*, *place pressure on*, *lead to* and *provide support*—without building giant phrase banks.

Topic vocabulary should begin with the actual question. Optional exploration may teach a small set of accessible, useful words and phrases in natural examples, followed by learner choice and use. It must not write the essay.


## 20.6 Paraphrasing, coherence and reference

Paraphrasing is not synonym replacement. Judge it through:

**MEANING PRESERVED? → NATURAL ENGLISH? → APPROPRIATE STYLE?**

Plausible alternatives may preserve meaning but sound awkward, remove important meaning while sounding natural, become unnecessarily complicated, work clearly, or succeed only partly. Judgement should lead to **Try it yourself**.

Teach:

> **COHERENCE IS NOT CREATED BY ADDING MORE CONNECTORS.**

Coherence comes from relevant progression, clear relationships, controlled reference, paragraph focus, development and avoiding unnecessary repetition. Connectors can express relationships; they cannot create missing reasoning.

Make reference words concrete by asking questions such as:

> What does “this” refer to? Is it clear?

Useful examples include *this problem*, *this approach*, *these students*, *this additional cost* and *such policies*. Explain how they help the reader follow the argument before adding technical labels.


## 20.7 Noticing, practice and independence

Extended Task 2 practice should frequently use:

**NOTICE → JUDGE → EXPLAIN → TRY**

Use short paragraphs rather than relying mainly on full model essays. A paragraph-noticing library may isolate one useful feature at a time, including clear development, concession, cause and consequence, realistic examples, good language with weak Task Response, connector overload, disguised repetition, conversational register, pseudo-academic language, simple language with strong reasoning, absolute and qualified claims, comparison, and active/passive focus.

Do not overload one paragraph with every feature.

Self-study modules should be short, focused, interactive, immediately useful, connected to real Task 2 writing and easy to leave and return to. A productive 10–20 minute investigation should let the learner notice and control one thing they did not consciously notice before.

An optional **Practice & Improve** layer may make relevant areas discoverable, but it must not become a giant compulsory menu or second linear course. Contextual entry points should arise from the learner's current work. After exploration, prompt immediate transfer back into that work.


## 20.8 Do not collect features

Use this permanent safeguard:

> **DO NOT COLLECT FEATURES.**

Learners are not trying to collect a passive, several complex sentences, a connector quota, advanced synonyms or a conditional. They choose language because it helps express the idea.

Language range means having choices and controlling them. A simple sentence, active voice, *but* or an ordinary precise word may be the best choice.

Before approving Task 2 learning content, ask:

1. What exactly is the learner learning to notice or control?
2. Is the difficulty in the thinking or only in the vocabulary?
3. Can a developing A2–B1 learner understand the task with available help?
4. Are weaker choices plausible and partly workable rather than caricatures?
5. Does the language point serve the writer's meaning?
6. Does the activity connect to one or more official Writing criteria?
7. Is any band-score or IELTS claim unsupported?
8. Is technical terminology genuinely needed?
9. Does help focus attention without taking over?
10. Can the learner try, revise and transfer the learning into their own essay?


# 21. Future Skills

Listening, Reading and Speaking require their own skill-specific pedagogy.

Do not assume Writing interactions automatically transfer to them.

Shared platform principles may include:

- learner action;
- purposeful interaction;
- progressive support;
- thinking before terminology;
- controlled answer/model access;
- diagnosis before help;
- feedback followed by another attempt;
- increasing independence.

Skill-specific pedagogy must be designed separately.


# 22. Final Pedagogical Test

Before approving an activity, ask:

1. What is the learner learning here?
2. What must the learner think about?
3. Does the interaction actually practise that skill?
4. Are cognitive and language difficulties being distinguished where necessary?
5. Is the support appropriate rather than excessive?
6. Are we presenting a recommendation as an IELTS rule?
7. Does feedback lead to another useful learner action?
8. Does support reduce as competence grows?
9. Could the learner eventually perform this without the support?
10. Is this digital activity genuinely better than simply giving the learner a worksheet?

If the answer to Question 10 is **no**, redesign the activity.
