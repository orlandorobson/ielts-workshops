### Ferrari · Choose the easy summary

**Paragraph A**

Key: **B**.

> Ferrari follows a business model that may initially appear unusual. Most manufacturers increase production when demand for a successful product rises, since selling more units normally produces more revenue. Ferrari, however, has historically placed considerable importance on maintaining relatively low production volumes. The company has described scarcity as part of the value created by this strategy. Restricting supply can mean that customers wait longer for a vehicle, but it can also contribute to the perception that Ferrari ownership is unusual and exclusive. In this context, producing fewer cars is not necessarily evidence of weak production capacity; scarcity can form part of what customers value.

Limited production can create exclusivity; the paragraph does not blame small factories or claim that maximum production always raises revenue.

**Paragraph B**

Key: **C**.

> This emphasis on exclusivity has contributed to a popular belief that Ferrari decides who is 'allowed' to own one of its cars. The reality is more complicated. Many Ferrari models are sold through its dealer network in the normal way, although demand and waiting times affect availability. At the same time, relationships with existing customers clearly matter: in 2024, about 81% of new Ferraris were sold to people who already owned at least one Ferrari. Access to some particularly exclusive products can be more restricted. Thus, the idea that money alone cannot provide immediate access to every Ferrari contains some truth, but the broader claim that Ferrari personally selects every owner exaggerates the situation.

Restricted access applies to some products, not every owner or every model. Neither unrestricted immediate access nor universal personal selection fits.

**Paragraph C**

Key: **B**.

> Stories about luxury brands often develop by simplifying a more complicated reality. A restricted model becomes evidence that 'Ferrari decides whether you are worthy'; limited production becomes 'Ferrari refuses to sell cars'; and a preference for established customers becomes a supposed rule excluding newcomers. Such claims can sound convincing because they contain recognisable elements of truth. The distortion occurs when words such as some, may or particularly disappear. A limited practice is then presented as a universal rule. Careful reading therefore requires more than understanding vocabulary: the reader must also notice the scope and certainty of what is actually being claimed.

The distortion comes from dropping qualifications from a partly true account, not from all stories being false or difficult vocabulary alone.

### Amouage · Reduce each paragraph to one simple idea

**Paragraph A**

Key: **A**.

> For most consumer businesses, success appears to depend on scale: produce efficiently, reach more customers and make a product that many people will accept. Niche luxury follows a less obvious logic. Amouage, a high-perfumery house founded in Oman in 1983, offers a useful example. Its own description emphasises finely crafted perfumes, creative integrity and a discerning clientele seeking products that feel unusual and personal. This does not mean that a niche house benefits whenever customers dislike its products. Rather, universal approval is not necessarily the main objective. A strong and recognisable creative identity may be more valuable than making every fragrance safe enough to please the largest possible audience.

Creative identity may matter more than universal approval. The founding date supplies background, not the explanation for success.

**Paragraph B**

Key: **B**.

> The word niche is sometimes treated as another way of saying expensive, but price alone does not explain the category. Niche products usually depend on some combination of specialist knowledge, distinctive design, controlled distribution, unusual materials and a clear creative point of view. In perfumery, this can produce compositions that are more demanding than familiar mass-market scents. Such distinctiveness involves risk: a fragrance with a strong personality may attract intense admiration from one customer and rejection from another. The commercial logic is therefore different from simply maximising the number of people who find a product acceptable. Selective appeal can help create identity, although scarcity or difficulty by themselves do not guarantee quality.

Price alone does not define niche; distinctiveness can attract and repel. The paragraph does not restrict all ingredients to rare natural materials.

**Paragraph C**

Key: **C**.

> Time provides another example of this unconventional logic. Industrial efficiency normally rewards faster production, yet Amouage publicly describes processes in which fragrance concentrates are left to macerate and mature before sale. The exact period is not the same for every product. Purpose, for example, is listed with four weeks of ageing, while Material is listed with fourteen weeks; the House's Essences use a much longer six-month double-infusion process in Muscat. Some Exceptional Extraits also use unusually high concentrations of perfume oil. These figures do not prove that a slower or more concentrated fragrance is automatically superior. Instead, they show how time, concentration and controlled production can become part of the craftsmanship - and therefore part of what a niche customer is being asked to value.

Ageing periods vary. Slow production or high concentration does not automatically prove better quality.

**Paragraph D**

Key: **A**.

> Amouage's Omani identity adds another layer. The company describes Arabian perfume culture as central to its work and presents Oman, historically associated with incense and myrrh, as more than a decorative background. Frankincense appears in numerous Amouage compositions, while manufacturing takes place at its Muscat facility. For an international customer, these references can make the product culturally specific rather than generic. This creates an apparent paradox: becoming more international does not necessarily require a brand to become less local. In a crowded luxury market, a recognisable connection with place can help distinguish one house from another, provided that heritage is developed creatively rather than reduced to a simple symbol.

A connection with Oman can differentiate the brand internationally. Removing local identity is not required, and frankincense is not named as the only source of identity.

**Paragraph E**

Key: **B**.

> The niche model nevertheless creates a balancing problem. A luxury house needs enough customers and international visibility to remain commercially successful, but excessive familiarity can weaken the sense of discovery or individuality that attracts some niche consumers. Similarly, unusual materials, long production stages and high concentrations can support a story of craftsmanship, yet none of them is valuable merely because it is costly or slow. The challenge is therefore not simply to remain small, nor simply to grow. It is to expand without making the product feel interchangeable with mass-market alternatives. Seen in this way, Amouage's position depends on managing a tension: international reach can increase the audience for a distinctive Omani identity, while the brand must still preserve enough creative difference for that identity to matter.

The challenge is growth without losing distinction, not avoiding growth. The passage makes no claim that the two categories use completely different ingredients.
